Prediction

Latent reasoning where the answer is a probability or a number.

Most latent-reasoning work is evaluated on questions with a discrete correct answer: a multiple-choice letter, an integer, a path through a graph. A forecast is not like that. The output is a number in the unit interval, it is scored against an outcome that has not happened yet, and the score is continuous in the answer.

Why a probability is a different case

A model emits one token at a time from a vocabulary of order $10^5$, which is about seventeen bits per step. For a discrete answer that is a generous budget: the answer is one of a few options, and seventeen bits carries it. For a probability it is not obvious that the budget is the binding constraint either, since a forecast reported to two decimal places needs only about seven bits. The question is what happens to the intermediate quantities.

A calculation that ends in a probability passes through odds ratios, base rates, weights on conflicting evidence, and partial sums. Each is a real number. When reasoning is written out in tokens, every one of those intermediates is rounded to whatever the model chose to print, and the next step reads the rounded value rather than the one the model computed. Continuous latent reasoning removes that rounding step. Whether this matters in practice is an empirical question, and it has not been asked directly.

Training against a scoring rule

Reinforcement learning for reasoning almost always uses a binary correctness reward. Damani and colleagues note the side effect: such rewards do not penalise a confident wrong answer, so they degrade calibration. Their method, RLCR, has the model emit a numerical confidence after its reasoning and adds a Brier score to the correctness reward. They prove that this, or any reward built on a bounded proper scoring rule, yields models that are both accurate and calibrated, and report that it “substantially improves calibration with no loss in accuracy” in and out of domain. Their summary of the contrast is direct: “while ordinary RL hurts calibration, RLCR improves it.”

Turtel and colleagues take the other route, training a 14B model with outcome-based reinforcement learning on questions drawn from a prediction market. Calibration improves as a side effect of training against real resolutions rather than as an explicit objective. The reward is outcome verification, not a scoring rule.

Both operate on token-level reasoning with a verbalised number at the end. Neither trains a continuous latent trace.

Probes against the stated forecast

Sarfati and colleagues trained representation-pooling probes on the intermediate activations of a forecasting model and compared them with the model's own output. Three findings bear on latent reasoning directly.

The probes are better calibrated than the stated forecast, on Eternis-Forecaster 8B and on two GLM models. Removing an influential source from the prompt “often changes the model's forecast while leaving the reasoning trace untouched”, and the probe activations track that shift, predicting its direction in 84% of cases including where the written reasoning conceals it. Forced answering shows that “forecasts are largely fixed before reasoning begins”: a single pre-reasoning pass recovers the committed answer and confidence, and routing on the spread of that pre-set distribution saves 30–47% of generated tokens with no loss of accuracy.

This is a measurement of the gap between what the internal state carries and what the text reports, taken on probabilities rather than on symbolic answers. It is one model family at 8B on one benchmark, and has not been replicated.

Reasoning and calibration

Whether producing a chain of thought improves calibration is contested, and the published results point both ways.

Yoon and colleagues compare six reasoning models with their non-reasoning counterparts across six datasets and find better calibration in 33 of 36 settings, attributing the gain to backtracking and exploring alternatives rather than to the presence of a chain as such. Mei and colleagues report the opposite for o3-Mini, DeepSeek R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet: verbalised confidence above 85% on wrong answers, with deeper reasoning making it worse, and introspection helping two of the three models and hurting the third. Hiremath and Hiremath describe a threshold beyond which additional reasoning budget induces overconfidence. Zeng and colleagues find that reasoning-oriented training raises verbalised calibration while lowering abstention rates on factual questions, and that within a trace, shorter reasoning correlates with higher confidence, which is the opposite sign to Yoon.

The sign of the effect depends on the model family, on how much reasoning budget is spent, and on whether calibration is measured from the text or from a probe. Sarfati and colleagues' forced-answering result is relevant to that last point: a calibration number read off the chain of thought may be describing something the model had already decided.

Measured forecasting accuracy

ForecastBench scores models against expert forecasters and the general public on a rolling set of questions, and reports that expert forecasters outperform the best model at $p < 0.001$. PolyBench collects 38,666 prediction-market snapshots and scores seven models on the 5,097 that were resolved and had both an order book and news context. Models may skip a market, and decisions below 0.6 confidence are discarded, so the reported figure is a simulated confidence-weighted return on the trades each model chose to make. Two of the seven come out positive on that measure and five lose money.

Earlier work is consistent with this. Schoenegger and Park entered GPT-4 in a live Metaculus tournament against 843 participants and found it significantly less accurate than the human median, and statistically indistinguishable from answering 50% to everything. An ensemble of twelve models did reach parity with a 925-person crowd on 31 questions in a preregistered comparison, a result about aggregation rather than about any single model.

Numbers through a token vocabulary

Separately from reasoning, there is a body of work on how numeric quantities survive tokenisation, which bears on the same question one layer down. Singh and Strouse show that forcing right-to-left digit tokenisation substantially improves arithmetic in frontier models and that the errors under left-to-right tokenisation are systematic rather than noise. xVal replaces digit tokens with a single embedding scaled by the value, and reports better out-of-distribution generalisation on scientific data. Number Token Loss adds a proximity-aware term to cross-entropy on the grounds that cross-entropy treats digits as unordered categories, and matches a dedicated regression head while staying at token level. Song and Bahri compare decoding numbers as text against pointwise regression heads and find the two match, with the decoder additionally able to represent smooth densities.

All of it concerns the output layer of a single prediction. None of it concerns intermediate reasoning steps.

Latent reasoning on structured data

Dudley and Oymak apply latent chain of thought to time-series and tabular prediction. A structured-data transformer compresses its query-position hidden states into feedback tokens, appends them to the input, and reprocesses, giving several rounds of latent computation before it predicts. Across 36 datasets this improves on the baseline for 7 of 9 time-series datasets, averaging 12.63%, and 23 of 27 tabular datasets, averaging 3.25%.

The comparisons are against a same-depth model without the chain, a deeper model matched on effective depth, and a weight-tied looped transformer. There is no comparison against the same reasoning written out in tokens, and the targets are point predictions rather than distributions, so the result does not separate the benefit of extra computation from the benefit of keeping that computation continuous.

What has not been done

Four things that would settle parts of this, none of which appears in the literature.

No one has run a latent chain of thought whose target is an explicit probability, quantile or distribution and compared it against the same reasoning emitted as tokens. Dudley and Oymak come closest and have neither the distributional target nor the token-reasoning baseline.

No one has trained a latent-reasoning architecture against a proper scoring rule. RLCR proves the result that would motivate it and applies it to verbalised token-level confidence. The latent-reasoning literature is motivated by search efficiency and does not cite calibration work.

No one has measured how much numeric precision is lost per decoding step when a continuous intermediate is written out. The argument that cross-entropy discards proximity between digits is made in the Number Token Loss paper, without an information-theoretic figure attached.

No one has shown that a model's internal representation of a quantity is more precise than the number it prints. Probes recover numeric value from hidden states, and Sarfati and colleagues show probes are better calibrated than stated forecasts, but the specific comparison of internal resolution against printed resolution has not been made.
