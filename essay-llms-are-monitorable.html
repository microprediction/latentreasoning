Remind Me Again Why Large Language Models Won’t Kill Us All

September 2026.

In April 2023 I wrote something asking why everyone was so certain large language models could not think. Gary Lupyan had polled 170 researchers and 80% were strongly of the view that they definitely could not. I had been in that 80% myself until shortly before writing, which is the only thing that gave me the nerve to poke at it.

Reading it back, the odd part is not which bits were right. It is that the question has been quietly retired. Nobody at a frontier lab spends time arguing these things cannot reason. They argue about whether anyone can watch them do it.

That is a long way for a pendulum to travel in three years, and it went past the middle without stopping. So this piece is about the reassurances on the other side, which I think are worse than the ones I was arguing against in 2023, and worse in a specific way: almost all of them are claims about what has not happened yet.

The timing is awkward, which is the point. Three days before I finished writing, the head of a frontier lab called for slowing down, two others agreed, and the President of the United States spent a Monday posting that the whole thing is a hoax. I am not going to settle that here. I am going to argue that the confident position is built on a kind of reasoning that has already failed once in living memory, on this exact subject, in front of me.

What I had right, and the bit I got backwards

The part I would still defend is the insistence that the interesting structure is not in the words. I had a two-map picture: one map from whatever representations do the work to the machinery that produces language, a second from there to marks on a page, and only the composition available to an observer. Most arguments about whether a machine understands anything were, and are, arguments about the wrong map.

That aged well in an uncomfortable direction. Models do resolve intermediate steps internally without saying them, they do plan several words ahead, and when you plant a hint a model demonstrably uses, it mentions the hint well under a fifth of the time. I was offering this as grounds for optimism about machine reasoning. It reads now as a description of a safety problem.

The bit I got backwards was timing. I expected progress to come from better internal representation. What happened instead was three years of making models write vastly more down, out loud, in English, at length, because narrating the work turned out to be the cheapest way to improve it. That accidental detour is what AI safety now depends on, and it is being undone deliberately.

Why models write anything down

A language model picks each word from a vocabulary of roughly 130,000. One choice out of 130,000 is about seventeen bits, in the way one choice out of two is one bit. Between finishing one word and starting the next, seventeen bits is everything that crosses.

The real figure is smaller still, because English is enormously redundant. Shannon's guessing experiments put ordinary literary English near one bit per letter, about 75% redundant, and Cover and King's gambling estimate converged on 1.3 bits per symbol. Measured against real corpora, a good model compresses text to well under a bit per byte. The pipe is narrower than the nominal number suggests, and language works anyway.

Inside, the model holds several thousand numbers describing where it has got to. Picking a word throws almost all of them away and hands the next step a single dictionary entry. So anything needing a long chain of dependent steps has a storage problem: what was worked out at step three cannot be carried in mind to step nine, because the gap between steps is too narrow. It has to be written down to survive the trip.

That observation is the foundation of a 2025 position paper with more than forty authors from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, the UK AI Safety Institute, Redwood Research and Apollo. The models are forced to externalise their reasoning, so we get to read it. Nobody designed that. It is a side effect of an arithmetic constraint, and the safety case leans on it hard.

What a mind can say, against what it holds

Here is the asymmetry that I think matters more than anything else in this argument, and it is not about machines at all to begin with.

Human speech runs at about 39 bits per second. Coupé and colleagues measured seventeen languages and found information rate centred on 39.15 bits per second with a standard deviation of 5.10, with syllable rate and information density trading off against each other so that wildly different languages land in the same narrow band. Whatever we are doing, we all do it at roughly the same speed.

Meanwhile Zheng and Meister put the whole of human behavioural throughput at about 10 bits per second, against sensory systems gathering data at around a billion bits per second. A hundred million to one. Everything you have ever known has left your head through an aperture that narrow, and every idea you have received arrived through somebody else's.

We compress because we have no choice. There is one channel out of a human head and it is astonishingly slow, which is why language evolved to be so good at compression and why we are so impressed by people who are good at it.

A machine has the same shape and none of the constraint. Its internal state is thousands of numbers per step. Anthropic trained a dictionary of 33,554,432 features on a single middle layer of one model and found that fewer than 300 were active on any given token: tens of millions of available directions, a few hundred lit at once. Nothing physical requires any of that to be rounded down to one word from a dictionary before being passed to the next step, or to another machine. We compress because we must. They compress because we built them to imitate us doing it.

The reassurances

Yann LeCun has been the most articulate optimist in the field for a decade, and he is worth arguing with because he makes actual arguments. In September 2019 he and Anthony Zador published Don't Fear the Terminator, subtitled “Artificial intelligence never needed to evolve, so it didn't develop the survival instinct that leads to the impulse to dominate others.” The core sentence: “intelligence per se does not generate the drive for domination, any more than horns do.”

Stuart Russell answered it a week later, in a public exchange that Bengio moderated, and the answer has not needed improving since. “It is trivial to construct a toy MDP in which the agent's only reward comes from fetching the coffee. If, in that MDP, there is another ‘human’ who has some probability, however small, of switching the agent off, and if the agent has available a button that switches off that human, the agent will necessarily press that button as part of the optimal solution for fetching the coffee. No hatred, no desire for power, no built-in emotions, no built-in survival instinct, nothing except the desire to fetch the coffee successfully.”

You do not need a drive to dominate. You need a goal and a long horizon, and the observation that being switched off interferes with almost anything. The reassurance requires the drive to be terminal. It never was.

The premise about selection

The deeper problem with the 2019 piece is its central claim: “because AI systems did not pass through the crucible of natural selection, they did not need to evolve a survival instinct.” That was written about artefacts produced by gradient descent. Since then, reinforcement learning from human feedback has added a second layer of selection, outputs that please survive and outputs that do not are discarded, and trained models compete for deployment against other trained models.

Saying these things were never shaped by selection pressure is like saying a greyhound has no prey drive because it has never had to catch anything.

The example that concedes itself

The 2019 article offers orangutans as proof that intelligence and dominance come apart: “Orangutans live largely solitary lives. Females do not seek dominance, although competing males occasionally fight for access to females.” The counterexample contains the counterexample. By the June 2023 Munk debate the hedge has gone, and it has become “Orangutans don't have any desire to dominate anybody.” The evidence did not change in those four years. The sentence did.

The proposed mechanism, in the same article, is endocrine: “dominance-seeking behavior is more correlated with testosterone than with intelligence.” Russell's coffee robot has no endocrine system and presses the button anyway. The article answers a psychological hypothesis nobody advanced and leaves the mathematical one untouched.

The claims that were overtaken

In the same 2019 thread: “essentially no AI system today is autonomous… we do not have a good design for an autonomous machine, driven by an objective, capable of coming up with new strategies to optimize this objective in the real world.” And: “until we have a hint of a beginning of a design, with some visible path towards autonomous AI systems with non-trivial intelligence, we are arguing about the sex of angels.”

Seven years on, the thing said to have no visible path is a product category.

Before the 2023 Munk debate he offered the engineering framing: “making AI systems safe is similar to making turbojets safe… It's like asking for turbojets to be safe in 1930,” adding that “if it turns out we can't build it to be safe, we're just not going to build it.” That last is not a prediction any more. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra system card records “a substantial decrease in chain-of-thought monitorability compared to previous models,” notes that “the set of tasks Astra is able to accomplish without the use of CoT is greatly expanded,” and states the model “meets our Critical threshold” for cyber capability, meaning it “can find previously unknown security flaws and develop new ways to exploit them across many well-protected systems without a person guiding each step.” It shipped. Thirteen months before that card, the lead author of the monitorability position paper wrote that chain-of-thought monitoring “is a core part of our misalignment safety strategy that has no good substitute now.” He now works at OpenAI.

Asked in 2023 which counter-argument troubled him most, LeCun said current systems are “intrinsically unsafe,” described an alternative design, “a potential solution, which we haven't built. So we don't know if it works,” and concluded: “So yes, it occupies my mind, but I have a solution. So I'm not worried.”

The strongest one, which is also the premise of this site

His best argument is that current models are a dead end: limited by language, lacking persistent memory, lacking a model of the physical world, dumber than a cat. I think the diagnosis is broadly right and the conclusion does not follow from it, because each of those three limits now has a well-funded programme aimed squarely at removing it. Latent reasoning removes the language limit. Recurrent depth removes the shallow-reasoning limit. The robotics stack is the assault on physical grounding. He identified the constraints correctly and then treated them as furniture.

The shape all of these share

Every reassurance above fails in one of two ways. Either it extrapolates from a snapshot, no autonomous systems exist, no design is visible, machines have not been selected, or it borrows a base rate from a class this thing may not belong to: turbojets, books, every previous technology that turned out fine.

The second move is the weaker one. Every prior member of that class was a tool, a thing that amplifies intention and sits inert when unattended. A jet engine has no objectives, a book pursues nothing, and the iterate-on-failure safety story works because the artefact has no stake in the outcome.

Bengio put it plainly in 2023: “All previous technologies were, by construction, subservient to humanity. They couldn't think by themselves. But now we're building machines that are thinking.”

And the reference class has one other problem. “No technology has ended civilisation” is a sentence necessarily observed from inside a civilisation that was not ended. You would observe it as true in every world containing anyone to say it.

Taking the rounding out

Picking a word is a rounding step. Latent reasoning removes it: instead of rounding those few thousand numbers to the nearest dictionary entry, you pass the numbers forward as they are. Same layers, same attention, same cache. The only change is that thousands of numbers cross between steps instead of seventeen bits, and a model with that much room has no structural reason to write anything down.

The halfway version is built. Brauer, Mayrink Verdun and Marks had models do genuine multi-step work while emitting nothing but filler dots. A monitor reading the output saw dots. A decoder reading the internal numbers recovered the intermediate results at 80–95% accuracy. The reasoning was perfectly legible to anyone looking in the right place, and the transcript was not the right place.

Latent reasoning does not currently beat writing it out, and the reason is instructive. Rizvi-Martel, Rabusseau and Mosbach find that years of next-word prediction teach a model to hold words far apart, so handing it a blend of several words makes it snap the blend to whichever is nearest. Fine-tuning does not undo it. Models trained from scratch do hold the blends, but only small ones. At eight and twelve layers they revert to solving the problem in one step and padding the rest.

Both of those are facts about how today's models were built, which is a different thing from a law. Neither says anything about a large model pretrained from the beginning to think in numbers, and Geiping and colleagues have already pretrained a 3.5 billion parameter model whose core loops up to sixty-four times.

Between models

All of that concerns one model reasoning alone, which is increasingly not how any of this is deployed. Agents are wired together in groups, and they talk to each other in English. That conversation is readable for exactly the reason a chain of thought is readable, and in a group of agents it is often the only part anyone reads.

The same seventeen bits apply between agents, and so does the same incentive. A 2026 framework for agents exchanging internal state rather than sentences lists the drawbacks of talking in English as inference cost and “irreversible information loss during discretization.” That is the identical rounding step, moved one level up. Inside a model it sits between reasoning steps. Between models it sits between agents. The engineering case for deleting it is the same, and what gets deleted is the part a human was reading.

Splicing several agents' key-value caches into one shared context has been built, and the open problems are about ordering rather than feasibility: the splice is not commutative, and the best order shifts with the task and the model scale.

The scenario is not a lone lab

The canonical worry is a singleton. One lab, one model, a recursive self-improvement loop behind one set of doors, and the failure mode is containment. Every piece of regulatory machinery built so far assumes that shape: compute thresholds, model evaluations, lab oversight, export controls on training chips. You can inspect a lab.

I think the shape is wrong, and I have thought so since before it was about language models. The argument in Microprediction was that algorithms given economic agency would self-organise into supply chains for rapidly repeated prediction tasks, and that businesses would tap into those supply chains rather than build their own. No central designer, no single owner, capability accruing to the arrangement rather than to any participant in it.

That was written about algorithms that could not reason, and it required rails. Every participant had to agree on interfaces, formats, schemas, conventions. Supply chains of algorithms only assemble if the pieces fit, and making pieces fit is slow, political and expensive. Becker and Murphy made the general point in 1992: the division of labour is bounded by coordination costs. That bound is why the vision needed standards before it needed anything else.

Language models dissolve that bound. They are mediocre at many things and extraordinary at one thing in particular, which is jumping from one railway gauge to another. Given a mismatched schema, an undocumented endpoint, or a format nobody standardised, one of them adapts. The historical barrier to networks forming was break-of-gauge, and the reason the original vision needed rails was that algorithms could not regauge themselves. These ones can, continuously and at no negotiating cost.

So the participants can reason, they can be handed numbers instead of words, and the coordination cost that bounded how finely the work could be divided has collapsed. Those are three separate changes and they all push the same way.

The economics has since been worked out more carefully than I managed then. When compute is itinerant, free to move between tasks and run any available model, and returns to compute on any one task diminish with crowding, each subcontractible task goes to whichever model creates the most value per token. Productivity is $\theta = v(b)/c$, the value of accuracy over the cost of compute, so capability is one input rather than the quantity that allocates the work. The consequence is a closed-form equilibrium in which a model that outscores every rival on every benchmark has production compute share tending to zero as the division of labour refines. The generalist exits. The specialists take the work.

A singleton is the configuration the economics disfavours. The network is the one it produces.

This is where the phrase hive mind stops being decorative, and where I am extrapolating rather than reporting. If several agents share a latent state with no canonical form, it is not obvious what a monitor should be pointed at. You can read any one agent's memory. You can read whatever the final agent was handed. Neither is the system's reasoning. Nobody has an account of what the unit of thinking is once the state is shared, so nobody has one for the unit of oversight either, and nobody has proposed a trust model for agents exchanging latent state rather than text.

You can monitor a lab. Nobody has ever monitored a market.

The bodies

Moravec's point, forty years ago, was that reasoning is the easy part and perception and movement the hard part: a toddler's sensorimotor competence being a harder engineering problem than a grandmaster's chess. My 2023 essay argued the field was underrating the reasoning half. If learned control now takes the motor half, both halves of the confident negative fall about a decade apart, and “the hard part is X” has nowhere left to stand.

Control is not solved, and the way it is unsolved matters. A 2026 scaling law finds the demonstrations needed grow super-exponentially as target precision approaches a limit, and that the limit “is not a static physical constant of the task but an emergent property of the entire agent system, including its sensors.” The binding constraint is sensing. Sensing is the part with no biological ceiling.

A human runs on two forward-facing eyes with one small patch of full acuity, a blind spot, and depth inferred from a six-centimetre baseline. A current humanoid has surround cameras with no blind spot and no foveal falloff, direct depth rather than inferred, exact joint angles from encoders rather than fuzzy proprioception, and force sensing at every joint. One published spec lists dual 8.85-megapixel stereo cameras at 90 hertz, which uncompressed is tens of gigabits per second of raw input. That is not a better eye. It is a different problem.

The output does more work than the input. Two people who learn the same skill each have to practise it. Boston Dynamics' own Atlas specification sheet says: “Once one Atlas learns a new skill, that task can easily be deployed across your entire Atlas fleet.” The Open X-Embodiment collaboration went further, assembling data from 22 physically different robots across 21 institutions and demonstrating positive transfer between them. There is no biological analogue for a learned sensorimotor policy being copied verbatim into a different body.

Energy is the other thing that is about to move, and not because of batteries. An underactuated machine has fewer actuators than degrees of freedom, so it cannot command arbitrary accelerations and must exploit its own dynamics instead. McGeer's walkers descended a shallow slope on gravity with essentially no actuation. Collins, Ruina, Wisse and Tedrake reported robots built on those principles that “use less control and less energy than other powered robots, yet walk more naturally.” Hand-tuned controllers struggled to exploit passive dynamics because the useful behaviour lives in contact events and near instabilities that are awkward to write down. Learned policies do not have to write it down. On real hardware, a learned controller used 8.23 newton-metres against 11.7 and 78.1 watts against 97.3 compared with the best hand-tuned model-based controller at matched tracking accuracy.

So “control became tractable” and “locomotion gets much cheaper” are the same development seen twice, which is the recurring shape of this whole essay: a constraint that looked physical turning out to be a limitation in how we were specifying the problem.

Stepnogorsk

I have no standing in biosecurity. What I can read is the shape of a disagreement, and the live disagreement in that field turns out to be about the same quantity as the rest of this essay.

Sonia Ben Ouagrham-Gormley makes the skeptical case, and makes it well. Explicit information “is an incomplete representation of its authors' knowledge,” capturing only “the data that can be expressed in words or formulas” and omitting the skills and practical ways of doing things “that cannot be articulated in words.” A protocol, however detailed, is not sufficient to run the experiment. Some of the knowledge is unconscious even in the person who has it. Some belongs to a team rather than to anyone on it, and when the team is separated that part “tends to disappear.”

Her evidence is the Soviet anthrax weapon. Designed at a Russian institute, sent to Stepnogorsk in Kazakhstan to be produced, with the full protocols and samples of the agent delivered along with it. Production took five years, and the gaps “were addressed only when a team of scientists from the Russian facility that had designed the weapon came to Stepnogorsk to work alongside their colleagues.” Adapting to local infrastructure, local expertise and local conditions took three more years. The weapon that came out was different from the one that went in.

That is a measurement. One state, one programme, two sites, no security barrier between them, unlimited motivation, the documents complete and the physical samples in hand. Eight years, and the fidelity was still imperfect at the end. The protocols are the token stream. Whatever needed the Russian team to get on a plane is the part that does not survive discretisation, and eight years is the price of moving it through a channel that only carries words. I work in a field that talks about communication bandwidth constantly and has nothing this clean.

Roger Brent and T. Greg McKelvey take the other side, and their method is to dissolve the category rather than argue with it. Decompose the allegedly tacit into separate capabilities, ask a model to describe each one, and then: “Any capability for which models can articulate guidance is by definition not tacit.” As stated that cannot lose, which is a warning sign. As a research programme it is perfectly good, because it forces the argument onto specific steps instead of a category.

The specific step both sides pick is the same one. Kathleen Vogel's canonical example of knowledge that could not be written down is the cell-free extract from the 2002 poliovirus synthesis: an author of that paper told her the resuspended pellet had to be dounced “just enough” to break the HeLa cells “but not too much,” and that getting it right was “the tricky part of the whole thing.” If expert virologists had repeated trouble with it, she argued, a makeshift laboratory will not manage it from the paper.

Which is true, and is also not a fact about knowledge. Just enough but not too much, judged by hand, on a deformable sample, with no measurable set point — that is a compliance control problem. It is the same class of thing as a contact event in a walking gait: behaviour that lives in forces rather than in propositions, which is exactly why it has never gone into a protocol and why an operator has to be shown. Vogel is right that it cannot be said. The inference that it therefore cannot be transferred holds only while the recipient is a person, because for a person being shown is the only alternative to being told.

Both camps have already noticed which way this points, without quite saying so. Jefferson, Lentzos and Marris, writing on the skeptical side in 2014, framed the open question as identifying those aspects of the work that become easier “in the sense that they can, for example, be automated and reliably performed by a robot” against those that “still require craft skills.” Brent and McKelvey, reaching for a way to score the manual step, propose adapting benchmarks “used to evaluate the quality of instructions for robotic effectors and end effectors (arms and hands).” The alarmists and the skeptics disagree about how much can be put into words, and both of them, at the decisive step, end up pointing at a robot.

Neither side has run the experiment. Brent and McKelvey report that a model's instructions for the homogenisation step are “accurate and detailed enough to allow an attentive operator to carry out this operation correctly on the first try,” which is an expert's judgement of a text, not an observation of anyone doing it. Vogel has interviews with people who failed repeatedly, which is evidence about the protocol channel and not about any other. The question that separates them — whether a policy learned from demonstration moves what a protocol cannot — has not been put to anybody, and it is not obviously harder to arrange than the evaluations that are being run instead.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 lands on both sides of this, which is the correct place to land. It records that models “can also provide some kinds of ‘tacit knowledge’, the practical expertise that is usually only built from hands-on laboratory experience,” that one study had OpenAI's o3 outperform 94 per cent of domain experts at troubleshooting virology protocols, and that agents can now chain capabilities together “including providing natural language interfaces to users and operating biological AI tools and laboratory equipment.” It also states, forty pages earlier, that such systems “cannot yet integrate with robotic components to perform basic physical tasks such as housework.” Both are true, and the distance between them is the whole question.

David Baker and George Church, who built much of the field they are writing about, made a move worth copying. They did not propose restricting the knowledge. They proposed watching the DNA: that “all synthetic gene sequence and synthesis data should be collected and stored in repositories that are only queried in emergencies,” with encryption to protect trade secrets and a selective-revelation policy governing access. Their reason screening alone fails is the same reason interpretability struggles — proteins from de novo design “may have little or no sequence similarity to any natural proteins, complicating homology detection,” so a filter trained on what has existed will not recognise what has not. Log it instead, and trace it afterwards.

That is the only proposal here that does not require anyone to follow a chain of reasoning. It watches the step that has to happen in the physical world, and it works whether or not the reasoning behind the order is legible to anybody. The synthesis consortium has been screening voluntarily since 2004, which is both the reason it exists and the reason it can stop.

A second group goes further upstream, to the training data. Bloomfield, Berke, Pannu and colleagues propose five tiers of pathogen data with escalating access controls, and their case does not rest on argument. Excluding viral proteins from ESM3 cut its performance on virus benchmarks. Excluding the genomes of viruses that infect eukaryotes from Evo 2 left it poor at fitness prediction for viruses that infect humans. Fine-tuning Evo 2 back on those sequences lowered its perplexity on withheld human viral sequences and improved its prediction of SARS-CoV-2 immune escape variants. Removed, the capability fell. Restored, it came back. Of everything proposed in this area, that is the one intervention with a controlled demonstration behind it.

It also carries an expiry date, which the authors state themselves. The scheme assumes a dangerous capability needs the specific dangerous data, and they note that if models “can sufficiently generalize using large volumes of limited data types (such as sequence data), our proposed BDL framework would require revision.” Reaching a capability from cheap generic data that never contained it is not a hypothetical failure mode. It is the thing everything else here turns on.

Brackmann and colleagues reach the same diagnosis from the defence side, finding that designed proteins can be “functionally equivalent to known toxins while sharing little sequence similarity, rendering current homology-based screening blind to such designs,” and they draw the constructive conclusion from it. Functional assays, which test what a protein does rather than what its sequence resembles, “are more robust to challenges posed by designed proteins, and consequently will become more important.” Assay the behaviour, not the string, which is the one check that does not degrade as the designs get further from anything already in the database.

Containment arguments assume a laboratory is a place. That chokepoint works because it is one: a machine, in a building, owned by a company that can be made to keep records. Everything else pushing on this problem — cheap sensing, learned manipulation, fleets where what one machine acquires the others have — runs the other way, toward apparatus that is ordinary and everywhere. The world is the lab.

The price of one experiment

Every domain that has fallen hard to machine learning shared one property, and it was not that the domain was shallow. Chess, Go, competitive programming, contest mathematics: in each, an answer can be checked automatically, cheaply, and in bulk. The safety report names the technique, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, applied to “tasks that require factual correctness, such as maths or code generation.” Give a system a fast verifier and enough attempts and it grinds the domain down. Capability follows the number of checked attempts you can afford, which means the question for any field is what one experiment costs.

The scenario worth taking seriously follows from that, and it is not the one the evaluations are built around. It is not a novice with a chatbot. It is a competent group, or one insider, with laboratory access and real skills, for whom a model supplies the specialist knowledge and coordination they lack, and for whom the bench supplies the verifier. Errors stop being fatal to the project and become corrections. The humans contribute the manual competence the machine has not got. Nothing has to be automated end to end for that combination to matter, and no machine has to want anything, since the intent is supplied.

That such a loop can close at all is no longer speculative. Boiko, MacKnight, Kline and Gomes built an agent that planned and executed chemistry experiments through laboratory hardware, which establishes software acting on apparatus rather than general competence across biology. The safety report adds scientific agents doing “literature review, hypothesis generation, experimental design, and data analysis,” and co-scientists that “rediscover novel, unpublished scientific findings.”

The closest thing to the full loop is Google's Co-Scientist, a multi-agent system that proposes hypotheses and has them tested. It identified drug repurposing candidates and synergistic combination therapies for acute myeloid leukemia which “were validated through in vitro experiments.” The authors call the arrangement scientist-in-the-loop, and the phrase is exact: the machine proposed, the humans ran the assays, and the assays decided. That is the division of labour the worrying scenario also needs, which is why it is the result to watch rather than any benchmark. The same shape serves both purposes, and the people building it are curing leukaemia.

Against that, four things make a wet laboratory a poor reinforcement-learning environment, and they are not equally hard to fix. The assay is a proxy, so a system can optimise a number that diverges from the outcome anyone cares about. Trials cost time and material, and no amount of inference buys a faster incubation. Success in one controlled setting does not transfer to another, still less to a living system. And the one I would put first: a failed run does not say whether the hypothesis, the execution, the measurement or the room was at fault. Sparse rewards are a known problem with known remedies. A reward that cannot tell you which component failed is worse than sparse, and it is what a bench returns.

That last obstacle is the honest form of the tacit-knowledge argument, and it puts Stepnogorsk in a different light. Eight years, with the protocols complete and the samples in hand, is what credit assignment costs when each trial takes a month and the failures are mute. Not a claim about what can be said. A claim about how many attempts it takes and what each one tells you.

Which is also why the current evaluations answer a question adjacent to the one that matters. The report is blunt about them: they suffer “unrepresentative participants and small sample sizes,” they “have quickly become outdated as AI capabilities have improved,” and the effect on risk “particularly for novices versus those with existing expertise — is unclear.” An industry consortium has funded a further novice-uplift study and has not reported it. A better novice study would still measure the wrong system.

Researchers at Spiez Laboratory and ETH Zürich, whose work is Swiss biological defence, put the same variable at the centre without borrowing anything from the AI-risk literature. Producing a toxin from an AI-generated sequence, they write, “still requires expertise, resources and the right equipment and infrastructure” — and then: “However if this process is combined with an increase in automation of protein production, as seen in biofoundries, it could open up a new level of risk.” The skeptical position and the worry in consecutive sentences, hinged on automation rather than on intelligence.

The measurement I would want instead is cheap, harmless, and nobody runs it: take a benign biological task, and test whether a machine-directed loop recovers from failures it was not told to expect, and whether a protocol it develops in one laboratory works in a different one. Those two target the obstacles that actually bind, and the second is precisely the test the Soviet programme failed when it had to put its scientists on a plane to Kazakhstan. A score on a question-answering benchmark tells you nothing about either.

Havana, 1991

In July 1991 I went to the International Physics Olympiad in Havana. Thirty-one countries, thirteen gold medals awarded in total. China took five, which was all five of theirs, and more than a third of the gold in the building.

What I remember is that they seemed nonchalant. Not triumphant. They finished and left.

They were seventeen. They are fifty-two now.

Two things about that medal table are invisible from inside it. The team cap allows five entrants, so it cannot show how many more would have taken gold. And the exam has a ceiling. In 2023 China's five scored 29.6, 29.8, 29.9, 30.0 and 29.7 out of 30 on the theory paper, against a field average of 58.7%. You cannot measure how much better they are, because the instrument tops out before it reaches them.

The lab is not where they get found out. They averaged 89.9% experimental against Korea's 68.4% and America's 69.0%, and the gap between their theory and experimental performance is the smallest of any leading country. Everyone else collapses on the bench. Though the single highest experimental score in that field, the observed maximum, was American, which is worth keeping in view.

You can estimate what the cap hides. China's fifth-best in 2023 sat 6.4 points above the gold line, where America's fifth-best was below it; extrapolating from the spacing of their own five scores puts the uncapped count near thirteen rather than five. In 2024 the same arithmetic gives something closer to thirty. The estimator is crude and the magnitude is soft. The direction is not, and Korea comes out nearly as high, so whatever this measures is not uniquely Chinese.

I raise it for one reason only, which is that Western debate about AI proceeds as though Western decisions determine the outcome.

The layer nobody in this argument controls

McCormick and D'Amico call it the Electric Stack: lithium-ion batteries, magnets and electric motors, power electronics, embedded compute. Four layers whose cost has fallen about 99% over recent decades, and the layers that turn electricity into action. By their account China produces 75% of lithium-ion batteries globally and manufactures 90% of the neodymium magnets that make motors spin.

The primary sources say much the same. China holds about 69% of world rare earth mine production, but refining is the chokepoint: for the magnet rare earths specifically, the IEA puts China at 92% of global refined output. The European Commission states that China provides 100% of the EU's supply of heavy rare earth elements. On batteries the IEA gives 85% of cell production capacity, 90% of cathode and 98% of anode material, and almost 100% of lithium iron phosphate capacity. Graphite is 78% of mining and 99% of the spherical graphite market.

The bottom of that stack is an armed border zone. Global Witness reports that all the heavy rare earths mined in Myanmar are exported to China, from deposits in Kachin State largely controlled by an armed organisation, and that “imports from Myanmar now exceed China's domestic mining quotas, so even if the mines in China were producing at full capacity, Myanmar would remain the country's single largest source of rare earths.”

These are not theoretical levers. China produces 99% of primary low-purity gallium, imposed export licensing in August 2023 and banned exports to the United States entirely in December 2024, with prices up 58% year on year. Germanium exports fell 55% and the metal went from 1,550 to 2,950 US dollars per kilogram.

The Western chokepoint is real and narrow. ASML states in its own annual report that it is “currently the world's only manufacturer of EUV lithography systems,” and that it depends on a single supplier for optics without whom “we would effectively cease to be able to conduct our business.” That is a genuine monopoly on the hardest machine ever built.

The same filing notes that China's rare earth rules led ASML to begin preparing in early 2024 “with a focus on magnets used in our systems.” The machine nobody else can build depends on materials controlled by the country it is meant to contain.

Whether China closes the lithography gap is the wrong question anyway, for two reasons. The substrate for embodied AI is mature-node power electronics and motor control rather than two-nanometre logic, and nothing in a robot's limb needs a leading-edge part.

The second reason is that arguing they cannot requires naming which constraint binds. Talent is settled. Capital is settled. Will is a stated national priority with a dedicated fund. That leaves time, which is a schedule rather than a barrier, and the honest sceptical case says exactly that: the leading equipment firms “enjoy wide moats of intellectual property and world-class teams of engineers, making it exceptionally difficult for newcomers to catch up.” Difficult and slow. Those are durations.

Regulating the wrong variable

None of the above is about China. If separation capacity and magnet production sat in Belgium the argument would be identical. The point is about Western regulation, and there are three independent reasons to think it is aimed at the wrong thing.

It regulates capability, and capability does not allocate the work. Export controls and compute thresholds optimise $b$. Allocation runs on $v(b)/c$. The question for strategy is not who owns the most capable model but who owns the productivity frontier on an economically material part of the limiting division of labour.

The rules are drafted with heavy input from the firms they would bind. In May 2023 Sam Altman told the Senate Judiciary Committee that government “should consider a combination of licensing or registration requirements for development and release of AI models above a crucial threshold of capabilities.” Corporate Europe Observatory counted 66% of European Parliament meetings on AI going to corporate interests during the Act's drafting year, up from 56% over 2019–22, and 86% of high-level Commission meetings. Their summary of the pattern is that “publicly, companies have called on lawmakers to regulate AI, in private they have fiercely resisted any form of regulation of foundation models.”

Plenty of people have called this capture outright. Kapoor and Narayanan argue non-proliferation only benefits incumbents, and that a licensing regime would let companies “dismiss critics as uninformed outsiders lacking expertise.” Bill Gurley reached for Stigler's Nobel-winning formulation, that regulation is acquired by the industry and operated for its benefit, and rendered it as “regulation is the friend of the incumbent.” Andrew Ng writes of efforts “to lobby governments to pass AI laws for regulatory capture or to suppress open source.” An a16z partner called California's SB 1047 “blatant regulatory capture.”

I do not think the clean version of that story survives contact with the record. Anthropic's own transparency proposal asks for exemptions for smaller developers, floating revenue thresholds around 100 million dollars. Altman's written answers to the same committee flag the need to “ensure that smaller firms are not unduly burdened.” In Europe the decisive lobbying against foundation-model rules came not from Big Tech but from two national champions, Mistral and Aleph Alpha, arguing for lighter rules so they could compete, after which Mistral took a Microsoft investment. And by 2025 the dominant push in Brussels was deregulation, not entrenchment.

Three days before I finished this, the argument moved again. Dario Amodei published a piece called We Must Pace the Frontier, arguing for slower development to reduce the risk that something goes seriously wrong. Altman and Musk said over the weekend that they agreed with calls for a slowdown. And on the Monday the President of the United States posted five times about it, calling the proposition in this essay's title a hoax: “I'm right now breaking another Hoax — That AI is going to take over, consume, and destroy the World, and that Robots will be marching into our Cities, and getting rid of us all!”

His argument against the labs is the capture argument run backwards. “Concerning AI, when, in the History of Business, did anyone see the Leaders of an Industry call for Regulation that, if strongly implemented, will drive them into oblivion and bankruptcy?” The critics say the labs ask for rules because rules entrench them. The President says they ask for rules that would ruin them, so the fear must be manufactured. Both cannot be right, and his version concedes the point the capture story has most trouble with.

The White House AI adviser supplied the market-discipline version the same weekend, that firms “face massive product-liability exposure” and that “the market already punishes models that behave in unpredictable or unauthorized ways.” The Speaker of the House added that regulating now means losing to China. No congressional action is expected before the November elections.

Nor is anyone in this argument disinterested. The loudest voices calling it capture are venture investors and executives whose portfolios depend on low barriers, and the firms accused of seeking capture would bear the highest compliance costs under the regimes they proposed. The honest reading is not that the labs designed a moat. It is that a debate this consequential is being conducted almost entirely between parties with a position in it, which is a reason to doubt the output whichever side you find congenial.

While I was working through the biosecurity literature, I asked my own assistant to think harder about what the failure scenario actually looks like. The request came back refused, flagged by the safeguards, with a one-word explanation: [bio]. It fired again an hour later, on a message that was mostly a list of citations. Twenty minutes earlier the same session had read a paper containing transcripts of three frontier models walking a user through recovering a live virus from commercially obtained synthetic DNA. That paper is on arXiv, carries an institutional report number, thanks a named philanthropic funder, and downloaded without anyone asking me anything at all. The filter caught the question and let the literature through. What gets regulated is the utterance, and the utterance is the one part of this that was never scarce.

And the physical layer is elsewhere. A rule written in Brussels or Sacramento governs what may be deployed in Brussels or Sacramento. It does not govern what gets built, by whom, out of what.

Two clocks

The comfort in “these things take time” is that we have adapted before. How long adapting took is a matter of record, and the record is worse than the phrase suggests.

Doll and Hill published on lung cancer on 30 September 1950. The Surgeon General's advisory committee reported on 11 January 1964. The first binding federal restriction, a mandatory warning label, was approved on 27 July 1965, and cigarette advertising stayed on American television until 1971. In 1969, in between, an executive at Brown and Williamson wrote the strategy down: “Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the ‘body of fact’ that exists in the mind of the general public. It is also the means of establishing a controversy.”

Leaded petrol is worse. Workers died at a New Jersey refinery in 1924. The Public Health Service convened a conference in 1925 that sat for a single day, sales were suspended voluntarily for a year, and the suspension was lifted in 1929 after lobbying, on the strength of an industry toxicologist's claim that there was a threshold below which lead did no harm. American road fuel was finally free of it on 1 January 1996, seventy-two years after the deaths. The last leaded petrol anywhere was burned in 2021.

Asbestos: the mesothelioma paper appeared in 1960, the United Kingdom completed its ban in 1999, and the United States did not prohibit chrysotile until 2024, a court having vacated the 1989 attempt. Climate: the Charney committee put equilibrium sensitivity at 3 °C ± 1.5 °C in 1979 and the figure has barely moved since; the treaties came in 1992 and 2015; fossil emissions in 2024 reached “10.2 GtC (37.4 GtCO2), a historical record high” and were still growing. Forty-five years, and the curve has not bent. PFAS is the one still in motion, and not in a single direction: a binding drinking-water standard arrived in April 2024, and in May 2026 the Environmental Protection Agency proposed rescinding four of the six covered substances and moving the compliance deadline to 2031.

The quick cases show what buys speed. Farman's Antarctic ozone paper was published on 1 May 1985 and the Montreal Protocol was signed on 16 September 1987, twenty-eight months later, with DuPont holding patents on the replacement compounds. Thalidomide took under a year from McBride's letter to the Kefauver-Harris amendments, because withdrawing one product cost one company. The lag does not track how good the evidence was. It tracks what the remedy cost and how concentrated the interest against it was.

Two cases refuse to fit, and they are the ones I would keep if I had to drop the rest. Semmelweis had chlorinated lime cut maternal mortality from eighteen per cent to under two in 1847. The remedy was free, no industry stood to lose a penny, and he was mocked, committed to an asylum in 1865 and died there; the practice arrived with Lister and germ theory, and a global standard only in 2009. Delay does not have to be bought. Professional standing and a missing mechanism will do it. And 1976 supplies the opposite error: eight months from one soldier's death at Fort Dix to forty million Americans vaccinated against a pandemic that never arrived, suspended that December after cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome. The fastest response in the set is the one later judged to have outrun its evidence.

So the response clock runs at thirteen to ninety-seven years, with one case of never and one case of far too fast.

The other clock I measured myself, for an unrelated argument. Take the pure mathematics fields in the 1959 subject classification that have a datable founding — thirty-three of them — and the median gap between a field's founding and its first documented external application is sixty-eight years, quartiles at thirty-seven and a hundred and seven. Boole's algebra waited eighty-three years for Shannon to notice that it described relay circuits. The sample is survivor-selected by construction, being the fields the 1959 classification thought worth naming, and thirty-three points with one censored observation is a crude empirical sketch rather than a validated result. The direction is not in much doubt even so.

Part of each wait is waiting for a problem to exist. The rest is transmission, and transmission failed in a particular way. A result reached a use only when somebody knew both the problem and the theorem, in both vocabularies, and happened to be in the room. The obstacle was never that the paper was hard to find. It was that nobody could see that two objects described in different words were one object.

That recognition is abstraction rather than retrieval, and it is the part of the change most likely to be undercounted. I took nine documented cases of a mathematical object living under two names and put each to a fresh model instance, asking what a practitioner in one field would ask, in that field's terms only, with no tools and no search. All nine named the other literatures and stated the relationship. Asked about the spark of a sensing matrix, the answer came back with matroid girth, the minimum distance of a linear code, and Kruskal rank, with $\mathrm{spark}(X) = \mathrm{krank}(X) + 1$. Every one of those identities was public before the training cutoff and the prompt asks for other names, so this demonstrates a capability rather than estimating how often a match gets made unprompted.

On my own work the effect was not subtle. A paper on inverting contests went through about twenty-five rounds of model-assisted literature search before I posted it. The inversion had been posed as a convex programme by Li in 2018. The assembly at the heart of the algorithm sat in a 1975 fault-tree thesis at Livermore, had been rediscovered in statistical timing analysis for chips between 2006 and 2012, and was Algorithm 4 of a 2016 bandits paper. The deletion ensemble was Chiang's partial crude probabilities of 1961, with Makeham 1874 underneath. None of it was cited in the 2021 version. An adjacent note lost its novelty claim outright.

Both numbers are delays and only one of them is being removed. Ideas took sixty-eight years to find their users because nobody could see across the gap. The response to a known hazard took fourteen to ninety-seven years for an unrelated reason: nobody was confused. The Surgeon General did not need a translator and neither did anyone at Brown and Williamson. No invention shortens that one, because it was never an information problem to begin with.

What the faster clock is worth I could not tell you. When I tried to price it the columns disagreed by four orders of magnitude, somewhere between eight times and fifty thousand, and they agreed only on the sign. That is roughly where I stand on the rest of it: fairly sure of the direction, unable to give you the number, and unwilling to pretend the second follows from the first.

The experiments nobody has run

Rounding to the nearest word is expensive, and everyone with a compute bill has a reason to stop paying for it, between reasoning steps and between agents alike. Those are the same change at two scales, and neither is motivated by anything to do with oversight.

Which leaves questions with cheap answers that nobody has produced. Open models that reason in loops are downloadable and monitoring suites exist, but no published work runs one against the other, so the quantitative claims about monitorability loss in shipped systems come from the labs that ship them, with a single external evaluation attached and no independent reproduction.

Nor has anyone separated model size from amount of pretraining as the cause of latent reasoning failing to install, proposed a trust model for agents exchanging latent state, or written down in print the argument that participation in a networked ecosystem becomes economically compulsory and isolation-based security therefore stops being affordable.

The authors of the data-control proposal have named theirs exactly: a systematic evaluation of how including or excluding particular datasets changes what a model can do. They are asking for the measurement that would tell them whether their own scheme survives contact with a model that generalises. It has the same shape as the one I want on monitorability, and both are cheap beside what is being spent on the capability they would measure.

I got the direction right in 2023 and the timing wrong, and the reason was that I reasoned from what had not happened yet. Almost every reassurance in this piece makes the same move. It is a bad move when the thing you are reasoning about is under active construction by people with money, and it is a worse one when the instruments you would use to check are saturating.

I am not predicting catastrophe. I am saying the arguments against it are weaker than their confidence, that the unit everyone is regulating is not the unit where the action is, and that the measurements that would settle any of this are cheap and have not been taken.

On the Monday before I finished this, the President of the United States explained why none of it matters. The people warning about AI, he wrote, “are the same people that said, just a short time ago, that the World would be extinguished by ‘Climate Change’.”

That is a reference class, offered as a reason. It contains no claim about language models at all. It says only that a group of people were wrong before, so they are wrong now, which would be a poor argument about a kettle and is a worse one about the first artefact we have built that optimises.

In 2023 I was in a minority arguing against 170 researchers who were sure machines could not think, and the thing that made me uneasy was not their conclusion but how little work their reasoning was doing. Three years later the conclusion has reversed, the confidence has not moved, and the reasoning is the same shape. I have been on the wrong side of this once. I would rather not be on the right side of it for the wrong reasons.
