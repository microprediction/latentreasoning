Remind Me Again Why Large Language Models Are Monitorable

September 2026.

In April 2023 I wrote something asking why everyone was so certain large language models could not think. Gary Lupyan had polled 170 researchers and 80% were strongly of the view that they definitely could not. I had been in that 80% myself until a few weeks before writing, which is the only thing that gave me the nerve to poke at it.

Reading it back now, the odd part is not which bits were right. It is that the question has been quietly retired. Nobody at a frontier lab spends time arguing these things cannot reason. They argue about whether anyone can watch them do it.

That is a long way for a pendulum to travel in three years, and it went past the middle without stopping.

What I had right, and the bit I got backwards

The part of the 2023 piece I would still defend is the insistence that the interesting structure is not in the words. I had a two-map picture: one map from whatever representations are doing the work to the machinery that produces language, a second from there to marks on a page, and only the composition available to an observer. Speech is a doubly noisy, once-removed look at the thing you care about. Most arguments about whether a machine understands anything were, and are, arguments about the wrong map.

That has aged well, in an uncomfortable way. Models do resolve intermediate steps internally without saying them, they do plan several words ahead, and when you plant a hint that a model demonstrably uses, it mentions the hint well under a fifth of the time. I was arguing this as grounds for optimism about machine reasoning. It reads now as a description of a safety problem.

The bit I got backwards was timing. I expected progress to come from better internal representation. What actually happened was three years of making models write vastly more down, out loud, in English, at enormous length, because it turned out that making a model narrate its work is the cheapest way to make it better at working. My direction was fine. My sense of what would happen next was inverted.

Which brings me to the thing I want to argue about, because that accidental detour is what AI safety now depends on, and it is being undone.

Why models write anything down at all

Start with something concrete. A model picks each word from a vocabulary of roughly 130,000 options. One choice out of 130,000 is about seventeen bits of information, the way one choice out of two is one bit. Between finishing one word and starting the next, seventeen bits is all that crosses.

Inside, the model is holding several thousand numbers describing where it has got to. Picking a word throws nearly all of that away and hands the next step a single dictionary entry.

So anything needing a long chain of dependent steps has a storage problem. What the model worked out at step three cannot be carried in its head to step nine, because the gap between steps is too narrow. It has to be written down to survive the trip. More than forty authors from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, the UK AI Safety Institute, Redwood Research and Apollo built a 2025 position paper on that observation: the models are forced to externalise their reasoning, so we get to read it.

Nobody designed that. It is a side effect of an arithmetic constraint, and the safety case leans on it hard. OpenAI runs such a monitor in production and its GPT-5 system card reports catching deception in about 4.8% of responses from one model and 2.1% from another, at an estimated 81% precision.

Two things wrong with the comfortable version

The first is that seventeen bits only describes one of the two routes between steps. As a model writes, it keeps everything it computed at every earlier position, and can look back at all of it. That is the key-value cache, built for speed, but memory is what it is. A model at word fifty is not working from word forty-nine. It has a private record of forty-nine positions, none of it written down, none of it seen by a monitor.

The second is that people went and checked whether the written reasoning describes what the model did. Turpin et al. planted biases in multiple-choice questions, watched accuracy fall by up to 36% as models followed them, and found the stated reasoning never mentioned the bias. Chen et al. put a number on it for current models: a quarter of the time for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, 39% for DeepSeek R1. Given an exploitable shortcut, models took it in over 99% of attempts and admitted to it in under 2%.

A channel that reports the decisive fact a quarter of the time is worth keeping. Baker et al. caught cheating with it that was otherwise invisible. It is not the same thing as watching a model think, and the gap between those two descriptions is where most of the public conversation lives.

Taking the rounding out

Picking a word is a rounding step. Latent reasoning removes it: instead of rounding those few thousand numbers to the nearest dictionary entry, you pass the numbers forward as they are. Same layers, same attention, same cache. The only change is that thousands of numbers now cross between steps instead of seventeen bits.

A model with that much room no longer has any structural reason to write things down, which removes the thing the safety case was resting on. The halfway version is already built: Brauer, Mayrink Verdun and Marks had models do real multi-step work while emitting nothing but filler dots. A monitor reading the output saw dots. A decoder reading the model's internal numbers recovered the intermediate results at 80–95% accuracy. The reasoning was perfectly legible to someone looking in the right place, and the transcript was not the right place.

So why has this not happened

Because it does not currently work very well, and I should be honest about that rather than wave at a trend. Coconut, the method that named the idea, beats ordinary reasoning 97.0% to 77.5% on a synthetic puzzle and loses 34.1% to 42.9% on grade-school arithmetic. On larger Llama models it beats writing nothing at all, which is a lower bar than it sounds.

Rizvi-Martel, Rabusseau and Mosbach worked out why, and the answer is the training rather than the design. Years of next-word prediction teach a model to hold words far apart, so hand it a blend of several words and it snaps the blend to whichever one is nearest. Pretrained models collapse every blend they are given. The same design at random initialisation does not. Fine-tuning does not fix it.

Train from scratch, then. The same paper shuts that down too: from-scratch models hold blends only while they are small. At eight and twelve layers they go back to cheating, solving the problem in one step and padding out the rest to look busy.

Both of those are facts about how today's models happen to have been built, which is not the same as a law. Neither says anything about a large model pretrained from the start to think in numbers, and Geiping et al. have already pretrained a 3.5 billion parameter model whose core loops up to 64 times.

Models talking to models

All of that concerns one model alone, which is increasingly not the deployment. Agents get wired together in groups, and they talk to each other in English, which is readable for exactly the reason a chain of thought is readable. In a group of agents it is often the only part anyone reads.

The same seventeen bits apply between agents, and so does the same incentive. A 2026 framework for agents swapping internal state rather than sentences lists the drawbacks of talking in English as cost and “irreversible information loss during discretization”. That is the identical rounding step, moved one level up. Inside a model it sits between reasoning steps. Between models it sits between agents. The engineering case for deleting it is the same, and what gets deleted is the part a human was reading.

Where I am guessing

Splicing several agents' memories into one shared context has been built, and the open problems are about ordering rather than whether it works. Splicing is not commutative: the result depends on which agent goes first, and the best order shifts with the task and the model size.

Here is where I stop reporting and start extrapolating. If the shared memory has no canonical form, it is not clear what a monitor should even be pointed at. You can read one agent's memory. You can read whatever the last agent was handed. Neither is the system's reasoning. Hive mind is a loose phrase and I am using it because nothing more precise exists: nobody has an account of what the unit of thinking is once the state is shared, so nobody has one for the unit of oversight either.

Anthropic reports a privileged band of representations that can be put into words, with other computation running outside it and never verbalised. If something like that band is what monitoring reads, then the question to ask of any design is how much of its work goes through the band. Latent reasoning moves work out of it. Agents swapping state extend the outside across the boundary between models. Which is, I notice, my 2023 two-map picture with more models in it, and I am not sure whether to be pleased.

The experiment nobody has run

Rounding to the nearest word is expensive, and everyone with a compute bill has a reason to stop paying for it, between reasoning steps and between agents alike. Those are the same change at two scales, and neither is motivated by anything to do with oversight.

Which leaves a cheap question nobody has answered. Open models that reason in loops are downloadable today. Monitoring suites exist. No published work runs one against the other, so the only quantitative claim about monitorability loss in a shipped system is a lab reporting on its own model, unreproduced. Everybody arguing about how much oversight is being lost, including me, is arguing about a number that has never been measured.

I got the direction right in 2023 and the timing wrong. I would rather not repeat the trick.
