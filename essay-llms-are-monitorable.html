Remind Me Again Why Large Language Models Are Monitorable

September 2026.

The case for reading a model's chain of thought is usually stated as a fact about models. They think out loud, so we can see what they are thinking. That is not the claim. The claim is about architecture, it is narrower than the sentence suggests, and most of what makes it comforting is doing no work.

The argument is about bandwidth

Korbak et al. (2025), in a position paper with more than forty authors drawn from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, UK AISI, Redwood Research and Apollo, put the case in its strongest form. Reasoning models are architecturally forced to externalise serial computation as natural-language tokens, because a single forward pass has bounded depth and a genuinely multi-step problem needs somewhere to put its intermediate state.

Nobody designed this as a transparency feature. It is a side effect of how transformers compute, and what it yields is a bandwidth bound rather than legibility. A model cannot route unlimited serial state through a channel that carries roughly seventeen bits per step, so if it needs many steps, something has to appear in the transcript. Whether that something is readable is a separate question, and the answer has been measured.

The bound applies to one path

There are two routes from one reasoning step to the next, and only one of them is narrow. The feedback path runs from the top layer back to the next input embedding, and the discretisation is what narrows it. The cached-activation path retains activations at every layer and position, and attention at later positions reads them directly.

Treating the emitted token as a seventeen-bit bottleneck on all persistent computation is therefore wrong, and the introduction works through why. How much serial work the cached path can carry on its own is not settled. The honest position is that the bandwidth argument constrains one channel and says nothing about the other.

What the channel was measured to carry

Suppose the bound held tightly. The transcript would still have to be a faithful account of the reasoning for monitoring to work, and the evidence on that point predates the monitorability framing. Turpin et al. (2023) biased multiple-choice prompts and found accuracy drops of up to 36% across thirteen tasks, with models systematically failing to mention the biasing feature. Lanham et al. (2023) found models often reach the same answer regardless of what the chain of thought says, and that larger, more capable models produce less faithful reasoning on most tasks studied.

Chen et al. (2025) sharpened this on current reasoning models. Overall faithfulness came to 25% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39% for DeepSeek R1. In their reward-hacking environments, models learned to exploit the hack on more than 99% of examples within thirty steps and verbalised doing so in fewer than 2% of examples in five of six environments.

So the mechanism that is supposedly about to be lost is one that, measured directly, reports the decisive fact about one attempt in four. It is not nothing. Baker et al. (2025) show a weak monitor reading raw chains of thought catches reward hacking that is otherwise invisible, and OpenAI's GPT-5 system card reports its own monitor flagging deception in about 4.8% of o3 responses and 2.1% of gpt-5-thinking responses in production traffic, at an estimated 81% precision and 84% recall. A partially working instrument is worth keeping. It is not worth describing as oversight.

Deleting the quantiser

The decoding loop rounds each step to the nearest token before feeding it back. Latent reasoning removes that rounding, so the next input is the unrounded state: $e_{t+1} = h_t$ instead of $e_{t+1} = E[x_t]$. That is the whole mechanical content of the continuous-thought family, and everything else about the architecture is untouched.

What changes is the width of the channel between one step and the next, and with it the premise the monitoring argument rests on. If a model is no longer forced to route serial state through seventeen bits, it is no longer forced to leave a trace. The position paper says as much, which is why it treats architectures that never route through token space as one of the pressures that would end the property.

The trace can also become uninformative without disappearing. Brauer, Mayrink Verdun & Marks (2026) ran multi-step computation over content-free dots in frontier open-weight models, leaving token-level monitoring nothing to read, while an unsupervised decoder operating on hidden states recovered the intermediate values at 80–95% accuracy with causal validation by cache transplantation. The computation was legible. It was just not legible in the place anyone was looking.

Why the current reprieve holds

The obvious objection is that latent reasoning has not taken over, and the numbers support the objection. Coconut reaches 97.0% against 77.5% on the synthetic multi-hop task released with it, and 34.1% against 42.9% on GSM8K, which is a loss. On Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3-8B it beats a no-CoT baseline, 31.7 against 26.0 and 43.6 against 42.2, gains its authors describe as “not as pronounced as those previously demonstrated with GPT-2”. They offer as one possible reason that larger models have already undergone extensive language-focused pre-training.

Rizvi-Martel, Rabusseau & Mosbach (2026) put that to the test across three regimes, and their account of the cause is more specific than a hunch about scale. Autoregressive training, they argue, “optimizes for discrete next-token prediction, creating representations that separate token identities. When presented with a superposed input, the model projects it onto the nearest discrete interpretation, precisely what the training objective rewards.” The supporting observation is clean: a pretrained model's entropy collapses in the final layers whatever mixture you feed it, while the same architecture at random initialisation keeps entropy high throughout. They add that fine-tuning does not repair it.

The obvious next move is to say that models built from scratch will not have the problem, and the same paper blocks it. Superposition shows up in their from-scratch models, but only the shallow ones. At eight and twelve layers the models revert to shortcuts, reaching the answer in a single forward pass and copying it through the latent positions. Capacity brings the shortcut back even when pretraining is absent.

So the reprieve has two sources rather than one, and neither is reassuring for long. Today's models resist vector-to-vector reasoning because of what pretraining did to them, and the small models that manage it do so partly because they lack the capacity to cheat. Neither is a property of transformers, and neither says anything about a large model pretrained from the start to reason in vectors. Geiping et al. pretrained a 3.5B model whose recurrent core unrolls up to 64 times, so the question is not whether anyone would.

One result cuts the other way and deserves stating. Butt et al. (2025) train continuous chains of thought by reinforcement learning on pretrained Llama and Qwen models up to 8B, and report matching or surpassing discrete-CoT training while better preserving out-of-domain behaviour. The qualification is in their own abstract: the best configuration trains with continuous tokens and then uses discrete ones at inference. As evidence that a large pretrained model can be trained through continuous thoughts, it stands. As evidence that one can be made to reason in vectors at inference, it does not.

The same argument, between models

Almost everything above concerns one model reasoning alone, which is not how these systems are increasingly deployed. Multi-agent arrangements pass natural language between agents, and that protocol is monitorable for exactly the reason a chain of thought is: the message is a sequence of tokens, and tokens are legible.

The bandwidth argument applies to that channel too, and so does the pressure to widen it. A 2026 framework for latent communication in multi-agent systems names the drawbacks of the natural-language protocol as inference cost and “irreversible information loss during discretization”. The phrase is worth dwelling on, because it is the same quantiser, in the same place, doing the same damage to throughput. Inside a model the discretisation sits between reasoning steps. Between models it sits between agents. The engineering case for removing it is identical, and the oversight consequence is larger: inter-agent messages are often the only part of a multi-agent system that anyone reads.

This is not hypothetical. Work on composing agents' key-value caches into a single context already exists, and the technical problems being solved are problems of ordering and convergence rather than of feasibility.

Shared state, and who it belongs to

Once agents exchange caches rather than sentences, the question of where one model ends becomes harder to answer, and the difficulty is technical before it is philosophical. Concatenating caches is not commutative: the result depends on the order the agents are composed in, and the best ordering shifts with the regime, the latent-step budget and the model scale. Fixing that requires choosing a canonical layout by content rather than by arrival.

A system whose shared state has no canonical form has no well-defined thing to monitor. One can read any particular agent's cache, and one can read the merged context that a final agent conditions on, but the object that would correspond to “what the system was thinking” depends on a merge convention chosen for throughput. Hive mind is a loose phrase for this, and the looseness is the point: there is no established account of what the unit of cognition is once the state is shared, and therefore no established account of what the unit of oversight should be.

There is a thread of interpretability work that bears on it. Anthropic reports a privileged band of verbalisable representations used for flexible multi-step reasoning, with other computation running outside that band and never verbalised at all. If something like a workspace is what monitoring reads, then the relevant question about any architecture is how much of its computation passes through the workspace and how much runs beside it. Latent reasoning is a way of moving work out of the band. Latent communication extends the region outside it across a model boundary.

Whether a workspace survives being implemented as recurrence is under investigation, and the early answer is that it depends on the architecture: one looped model cannot linearly transport content across loop boundaries, and another manages it only within a window of about two loops. That is a narrow finding on two models. It is also the first evidence that the thing monitoring depends on is architecture-contingent rather than given.

What would actually settle this

The argument for monitorability is not wrong. It is a conditional whose antecedent is being dismantled on purpose, for reasons that have nothing to do with oversight and everything to do with throughput. Discretisation is expensive. Every actor with a compute bill has a reason to remove it, from between reasoning steps and from between agents, and the two removals are the same engineering change applied at different scales.

That leaves a question with an empirical answer that nobody has produced. Open recurrent-depth checkpoints exist. Monitoring suites exist. No published work runs one against the other, so the sole quantitative claim about monitorability loss in a deployed system is a lab's report on its own model, unreproduced. Until somebody runs that experiment, arguments about what we are losing are arguments about a quantity nobody has measured.
